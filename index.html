How I Work

What sets me apart is how I think about work.

I am strongly opposed to exploitative systems and obfuscation. I do not tolerate vague priorities, unclear ownership, or busywork disguised as productivity.

I like to understand the value of things, the cost of delay, and the risk created by missing or incorrect data. If something matters, I build calm, repeatable systems that protect executive focus, surface what matters early, and turn decisions into delivery.

If you want an assistant who brings rigour, taste, and operational leverage to leadership work, you are in the right place. Here's what that looks like in practice.

Approach 01

Time Architecture

I start with a quiet audit. Before restructuring anyone's calendar, I spend the first week mapping how time is currently spent: what gets scheduled, what runs over, what gets cancelled, and what never makes it onto the calendar at all. The goal is to identify where executive attention is being spent versus where it's most needed. From there, I build a system of briefings, buffers, and priority gates that protect deep work without creating bottlenecks.

Approach 02

Process Engineering

Every process I document follows a simple principle: if I disappeared tomorrow, could someone else pick this up and deliver the same result? I document as I go, not after. While I'm learning a workflow, I'm simultaneously building the reference guide for it. This means the ramp-up period doubles as the documentation period. By the time I'm comfortable, there's already a system anyone else could follow.

Sample Deliverable

Standard Operating Procedure

Trigger

  • What event initiates this process

Steps

  • Each action in sequence, with the tool used
  • Decision points flagged with if/then logic
  • Escalation path if something goes wrong

Output

  • What "done" looks like, with a quality check
  • Where the output lives and who gets notified

Owner & Review Cycle

  • Named owner, review date, version number

Built in Notion or your preferred tool. Written to be followed, not just filed. Reviewed monthly or after any process failure.

Approach 03

Stakeholder Clarity

Clear communication starts with knowing what decision your audience needs to make. I structure every update around three things: what's done, what's next, and what needs input. No long paragraphs, no unnecessary context. But the real work happens before the update. If I say something is done, the document is already uploaded, correctly named, in the right folder. The update is just confirmation. Nobody should ever need to reply asking "where is it?"

Sample Deliverable

End-of-Day Update Format

Completed

  • Meeting minutes uploaded to [shared folder]
  • Q3 report draft sent to [stakeholder] for review
  • Travel itinerary confirmed for [dates]

In Progress

  • Vendor shortlist, due [date], on track

Needs Your Input

  • Budget approval for [item], decision by [date]

Sent daily at end of working day. Blockers flagged in the morning while there's still time to solve them. Surprises at 5pm are worse than no update at all.
